To calculate the centripetal acceleration ($a_c$) of a person at the equator, we need to account for the Earth's rotation. Even though you feel like you are standing still, you are actually traveling in a massive circle once every 24 hours.

### 1. Identify the Known Values
* **Radius ($R$):** $6378 \text{ km} = 6,378,000 \text{ meters}$
* **Time Period ($T$):** One full rotation takes 24 hours.
    * $24 \times 60 \times 60 = 86,400 \text{ seconds}$

---

### 2. Calculate Angular Velocity ($\omega$)
Angular velocity is how many radians the Earth rotates per second. Since one full circle is $2\pi$ radians:
$$\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{2\pi}{86,400} \approx 7.27 \times 10^{-5} \text{ rad/s}$$



---

### 3. Calculate Centripetal Acceleration ($a_c$)
We use the formula $a_c = \omega^2 R$:
* $a_c = (7.27 \times 10^{-5})^2 \times 6,378,000$
* $a_c = (5.285 \times 10^{-9}) \times 6,378,000$
* **$a_c \approx 0.0337 \text{ m/s}^2$**



---

### **Summary for your Professor**
To explain this clearly, you can say:
> "To find the centripetal acceleration at the equator, I first converted the Earth's radius to meters and the rotation period of 24 hours into seconds. Using the formula **$\omega = 2\pi / T$**, I found the angular velocity. Finally, applying **$a_c = \omega^2 R$**, I calculated an acceleration of approximately **$0.034 \text{ m/s}^2$**."

**Interesting Fact:**
This acceleration is very small—only about **0.34%** of the strength of gravity ($9.8 \text{ m/s}^2$). This is why you don't feel like you're being "flung" off the Earth, although it does make you weigh slightly less at the equator than at the North Pole!
