To find the equilibrium position for the third charge $q_3$, we need to find the point where the net electric force acting on it is zero. Since $q_1$, $q_2$, and $q_3$ are all positive, $q_3$ will be repelled by both $q_1$ and $q_2$. Equilibrium occurs where these two opposing forces are equal in magnitude.

### 1. Set Up the Variables


* $q_1 = +4 \text{ C}$ (placed at the origin, $x = 0$)

  
* $q_2 = +9 \text{ C}$ (placed at $x = 2 \text{ m}$)

  
* $q_3 = +1 \text{ C}$ (placed at some distance $x$ from $q_1$)
  
* The distance from $q_3$ to $q_2$ is $(2 - x)$



### 2. Balance the Forces
According to Coulomb's Law, the force $F$ between two charges is $F = \frac{k |q_a q_b|}{r^2}$. For $q_3$ to be in equilibrium, the force from $q_1$ ($F_1$) must equal the force from $q_2$ ($F_2$):

$$\frac{k q_1 q_3}{x^2} = \frac{k q_2 q_3}{(2 - x)^2}$$

### 3. Simplify the Equation
Notice that $k$ and $q_3$ appear on both sides, so they cancel out. This means the equilibrium position depends only on the magnitudes of $q_1$ and $q_2$, not on the value of the third charge itself:

$$\frac{q_1}{x^2} = \frac{q_2}{(2 - x)^2}$$

Substitute the values for $q_1$ and $q_2$:
$$\frac{4}{x^2} = \frac{9}{(2 - x)^2}$$

### 4. Solve for $x$
To make this easier, take the square root of both sides:
$$\frac{2}{x} = \frac{3}{2 - x}$$

Now, cross-multiply:
$$2(2 - x) = 3x$$
$$4 - 2x = 3x$$
$$4 = 5x$$
$$x = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8 \text{ m}$$

---

**Final Answer:**
The equilibrium position for $q_3$ is **$0.8 \text{ m}$** away from the $+4 \text{ C}$ charge (or $1.2 \text{ m}$ away from the $+9 \text{ C}$ charge) along the line connecting them.
